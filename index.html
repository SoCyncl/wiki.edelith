Banana
Musa × paradisiaca and related cultivars

A banana is an elongated, edible fruit — botanically a berry — produced by several kinds of large herbaceous flowering plants in the genus Musa. In some countries, bananas used for cooking may be called plantains, distinguishing them from dessert bananas. The fruit is variable in size, colour, and firmness, but is usually elongated and curved, with soft flesh rich in starch covered with a rind, which may be green, yellow, red, purple, or brown when ripe.

The fruits grow upward in clusters known as hands, on a tree-like plant usually called a banana tree. A bunch of bananas is called a hand (or a bunch), and a single banana is called a finger. Each plant bears one bunch of bananas, after which the main stalk dies back. New stalks grow from the root mass to produce the next crop of bananas.

Worldwide, there is no sharp distinction between "bananas" and "plantains." Especially in the Americas and Europe, "banana" usually refers to soft, sweet, dessert bananas, particularly those of the Cavendish group, which are the main exports from banana-growing countries. By contrast, Musa cultivars with firmer, starchier fruit are called "plantains."

Etymology

The word banana is thought to be of West African origin, possibly from the Wolof word banaana, and passed into English via Spanish or Portuguese. It was introduced to the Americas by Portuguese sailors who brought the fruits from West Africa in the 16th century.

The scientific names Musa acuminata and Musa balbisiana are named after Italian botanist Antonio Musa, physician to the first Roman Emperor Augustus, and naturalist Luigi Balbis respectively. The genus name may alternatively come from the Arabic word mauz, a term for the fruit.

History
Origin and early cultivation

Musa species are native to tropical Indomalaya and Australia. The earliest domestication of bananas was carried out by Papuan people around 8000 BCE in New Guinea, based on evidence of cultivation in the Kuk Swamp archaeological site in the Western Highlands of Papua New Guinea. From there, cultivation spread westward to India, where references to the banana fruit date to around 600 BCE in Buddhist scriptures.

The earliest direct evidence of banana cultivation comes from Kuk Swamp, where archaeobotanical evidence suggests domestication of bananas in Papua New Guinea dating to between 8000 and 6000 BCE — making it among the earliest agricultural sites in the world.

Two wild species — Musa acuminata and Musa balbisiana — were domesticated thousands of years ago to produce the banana plants we know today. These two species were crossed, and modern dessert bananas (such as the Cavendish) are sterile triploid hybrids derived from M. acuminata with the genetic designation AAA.

Spread across the world

By the 3rd century CE, banana cultivation had spread to China, and by the 6th century CE to Japan. During the Arab expansion of the 7th to 8th centuries CE, bananas were spread westward to the Middle East, the Levant, and North Africa. By the 10th century, descriptions of banana cultivation appear in texts from Palestine.

Portuguese sailors brought bananas from West Africa to the Americas in the early 16th century. The fruit was brought to the Caribbean Islands by Portuguese explorers in the 1500s, and cultivation in the Americas began in earnest. The first major commercial development occurred in the Caribbean in the 1870s and 1880s.

Alexander the Great is said to have tasted bananas during his Indian campaign of 327 BCE — though later scholarship has questioned whether the accounts describe bananas or another fruit entirely.
Commercial history

In the 1880s, Carl Franzius began bringing bananas to the United States from Jamaica, eventually founding what became the United Fruit Company in 1899. The United Fruit Company became one of the most powerful corporations in the Americas, wielding enormous political and economic influence over governments throughout Central America — a phenomenon that gave rise to the term banana republic.

The original popular dessert banana, the Gros Michel cultivar, was commercially dominant throughout the early 20th century until the 1950s, when a fungal disease called Panama disease (caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense) devastated plantations worldwide. The Gros Michel is considered by many who tasted it to have had a richer, creamier flavour than the Cavendish banana that replaced it.

The Cavendish, resistant to the then-prevalent strain of Panama disease, was adopted as the commercial replacement in the 1950s and 1960s, and has since become the ubiquitous banana found in supermarkets worldwide. However, a new strain of Panama disease — Tropical Race 4 (TR4) — now threatens Cavendish plantations globally, raising fears of a repeat scenario.

Botany

Despite their tree-like appearance, banana plants are not trees. The "trunk" is actually a pseudostem formed by tightly packed leaf bases. The true stem is underground, called a corm, from which the pseudostem and leaves emerge. Each plant typically reaches 3–10 metres (10–33 ft) in height.

Banana plants are monocots. The leaves are large, up to 2.7 metres (8.9 ft) long and 60 centimetres (24 in) wide, and grow spirally from the corm. Each leaf unfurls from a cylindrical sheath that adds to the pseudostem. When the plant is mature, a large purple-red bract (the flower spike) emerges from the top of the pseudostem. This is called the banana heart or banana blossom.

The fruit itself is classified botanically as a berry, with the flesh derived from the ovary wall and the pulp from the placenta. Commercial banana varieties are parthenocarpic — they develop without fertilisation — which is why the common supermarket banana contains no seeds (or only vestigial, undeveloped ones).

Bananas are classified by genome group — reflecting the proportion of M. acuminata (A) and M. balbisiana (B) chromosomes — as well as by ploidy level (diploid AA/BB, triploid AAA/ABB, tetraploid AAAA, etc.).

Production

Bananas are among the most widely cultivated crops in the world. They are grown in more than 135 countries, primarily for local consumption, with only around 15–20% of production entering international trade. Bananas are a major staple food in tropical regions of Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia.

Commercial banana production is labour-intensive and relies heavily on plantation agriculture. Plantations are typically operated by large multinational corporations — most prominently Chiquita (formerly United Fruit), Dole, and Del Monte — though significant production is also carried out by smallholder farmers, particularly in Southeast Asia and parts of Africa.

Nutrition

Raw bananas (not including the peel) are 75% water, 23% carbohydrates, 1% protein, and contain negligible fat. In a 100-gram reference amount supplying 89 Calories, a raw banana is a rich source of vitamin B6 (31% of the Daily Value), and contains moderate amounts of vitamin C, manganese, dietary fibre, and potassium.

The ripening of bananas causes a conversion of starches to sugars, making ripe bananas sweeter and more easily digestible than unripe ones. Unripe bananas are high in resistant starch, which behaves like dietary fibre and may have prebiotic effects. The glycaemic index of bananas ranges from around 51 (underripe) to 57 (overripe).

Trade

Bananas are the world's most exported fresh fruit and the most traded fruit commodity overall. Global export trade is estimated at around 18–20 million tonnes per year, with a value of approximately USD $10–12 billion. The top importing regions are the European Union, the United States, Russia, and Japan.

The structure of the banana trade has historically been dominated by a small number of transnational corporations that control production, logistics, and distribution. This concentration of power has been a source of considerable controversy, particularly regarding labour rights, environmental practices, and political interference in producing countries.

Fair trade certification has grown as a response to concerns about the conditions in which bananas are grown and traded. The Fairtrade banana market represents a small but growing percentage of overall banana trade, with certified production concentrated in Latin America, the Caribbean, and parts of Africa.

Diseases

Banana plants are susceptible to a range of diseases, some of which pose serious threats to commercial production.

Panama disease (Fusarium wilt)

Panama disease is caused by the soil-borne fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc). Race 1 of this pathogen devastated Gros Michel production in the mid-20th century, effectively eliminating commercial cultivation of the cultivar. Tropical Race 4 (TR4), a more aggressive strain first identified in Taiwan in the 1990s, now threatens the Cavendish banana and has spread to South and Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America.

Cultural significance

Bananas hold deep cultural importance in many tropical societies. In Hindu tradition, the banana plant is sacred — its leaves are used as plates in religious ceremonies and offerings. In Buddhist mythology, the banana is one of the "trees of paradise." Banana leaves are used across South and Southeast Asian cultures for food preparation, religious offerings, and as biodegradable serving ware.

In popular culture of the Western world, the banana became an icon of physical comedy — particularly the banana peel as a slapstick prop — from the early 20th century onward. The image of someone slipping on a banana peel is one of the most enduring visual gags in comedy history, appearing in silent film, vaudeville, and countless cartoons.

The banana also became a symbol of American corporate imperialism following the United Fruit Company's influence on Central American politics. The term banana republic, coined by author O. Henry in his 1904 collection Cabbages and Kings, has entered the English language to describe a small, politically unstable nation dependent on a single export and dominated by foreign corporate interests.

In art, the banana gained an unexpected contemporary significance through Maurizio Cattelan's 2019 work Comedian — a fresh banana affixed to a wall with a strip of duct tape — which sold for $120,000 at Art Basel Miami Beach, generating widespread public debate about the nature and value of conceptual art.
